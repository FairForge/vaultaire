# 43.4 probe — the law-enforcement policy and "what we can produce" (Stage 1a Prompt 38, Part A)

Plan item 6b and 43.4 (`docs/IMPLEMENTATION_PLAN.md` § 43.4), Trust lane charter item 2
(`docs/surfaces/TRUST.md`), contract rules 7 and 8. Read 2026-10-24. The rule this note
serves: **a fact without a code source is not stated** — every sentence the pages say
about what we hold, keep or can hand over is a row of `internal/api/landing/produce.json`
(or of `modes.json`), held to its code by a test.

## 1. Whose shape we copy

### Signal — `signal.org/bigbrother/` ("Government Requests")

A list of every request answered, newest first, each with its court, date and a short
excerpt, and the whole record behind a link. What we copy: **the answer is the data
inventory, not prose.** Signal's sentences we take the shape of:

> "the only information we can produce in response to a request like this"

> "We've designed the Signal service to minimize the data we retain about Signal users"

Signal can say one sentence because it holds two fields. We hold more, so the page is
the field-by-field table that one sentence stands for. The per-request list is the
yearly transparency report (43.4's third artefact). It is not built: there is nothing
to list yet.

### Proton — `proton.me/legal/law-enforcement`

The sections are: about the company and its jurisdiction, what information is available,
the user notification policy, how to request assistance, what a request must contain, how
data is disclosed, and changes to the policy. We copy **the jurisdiction stated first**
(ours is the United States; the plan says "no Swiss cosplay"), **user notice as its own
section**, and **refusing overbroad requests**:

> "targeted users will eventually be informed and afforded the opportunity to object"

> "We'll be unable to inform about or preserve data in relation to overly broad or vague requests"

Proton does not break its available data down per product. We do: that is the produce page.

### Ente — `ente.com/privacy`

Ente has no law-enforcement page. Its privacy policy § 7(b) discloses "to comply with any
court order, law, or legal process" and "to respond to any government or regulatory
request", and § 1 says files *and their metadata* are stored encrypted. What we take
from it is **the contrast**: under SSE-C our object names and metadata are NOT encrypted
(proved below), so we may not borrow Ente's metadata sentence.

### Apple — Legal Process Guidelines (US, published October 2025)

These are the guidelines' sections. Part II, Service of Legal Process: A. subpoenas,
warrants and orders; B. managing and responding to them; C. witness testimony;
D. Preservation Requests; E. Emergency Requests; F. Account Restriction/Deletion;
G. Customer Notice. Part III, Information Available from Apple, is one subsection per
service, and each says what exists, how long it is kept and which process yields it.
We copy **the per-service "what is available, how long it is kept" structure** (our
"service" is the bucket mode and the account) and **the end-to-end carve-out stated as a
key fact**:

> "Apple does not receive or retain encryption keys for customer's end-to-end encrypted data."

> "Apple will notify customers when their Apple account information is sought in response to legal process"

> "Apple is permitted, but not required, to voluntarily disclose information … if Apple
> believes in good faith that an emergency involving imminent danger of death or serious
> physical injury to any person requires such disclosure without delay."

Apple's preservation section ("a one-time data pull … for 90 days") is the one we
**cannot** copy. See the gap below (RP53-11).

### Exactly scoped: what a Private (SSE-C) bucket still gives us

On 2026-10-24 a probe wrote `holiday/photo.jpg` with an SSE-C key and the header
`x-amz-meta-trip: paris-2026` into an `ssec_required` bucket. Its `object_head_cache` row:

```
"object_key":"holiday/photo.jpg","size_bytes":15,"etag":"db7f6a751bba4d2caed3ec862bff7bf2",
"content_type":"image/jpeg","metadata":{"trip": "paris-2026"},
"encryption_algorithm":"AES256-SSE-C-v3","ssec_key_md5":"8NB6psqPvuXCjIqE3J2m5Q==", …
```

`md5("hello plaintext")` = `db7f6a751bba4d2caed3ec862bff7bf2`. So:

- **Object keys (names) of an SSE-C bucket are NOT encrypted.** Neither are the size, the
  content type or the user metadata.
- **The ETag of a single-request SSE-C upload is the MD5 of the plaintext.**
  `sse_stream.go`: "the MD5 tee stays before it (the ETag is the plaintext's)". With it
  we, or anyone holding our database, can confirm whether a known file is stored. AWS's
  SSE-C ETag is not the plaintext MD5 (AWS documents that SSE-C and SSE-KMS ETags are not
  an MD5 of the object data). A multipart SSE-C upload's `-N` ETag was not probed, so the
  page states the single-request case only.
- **The MD5 of the customer's key is stored** (`ssec_key_md5`, migration 094) so that a
  wrong key is refused from the row.
- **The key is in no column** (the test searches the row's JSON for the key's base64 and
  hex) and **the plaintext is in no stored byte** (the test reads every file the local
  driver wrote).

`modes.json`'s Private sentence said "We still see object names, sizes, counts,
timestamps and when they are read". It left out the content type, the metadata, the
plaintext MD5 and the key MD5. That is finding **RP53-10**, fixed in the same PR, and
`TestPrivateModeSentenceNamesWhatTheRowHolds` now requires those items.
